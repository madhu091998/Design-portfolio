Sales teams don't only pay for revenue. Many also reward goals like getting certified or running good demos, called MBOs. Everstage customers managed these in spreadsheets and email.

I designed MBO across both phases, first the programs and views, then approvals and locking, working closely with our PM, engineering and customer-facing teams.

What's an MBO?

MBO stands for Management by Objectives. These are goals that can't be measured as a sales number, so a manager scores them at the end of each period, which could be a month, a quarter, half a year or a full year. The score decides part of the person's payout, typically 10 to 35%.

One example runs through this case study:

Alex's goal is to run 10 product demos this quarter. Jordan, Alex's manager, scores it at the end. A score of 80 or more pays a $500 bonus.

Who uses MBO?

  • Admins

    Comp or RevOps teams who set up MBO programs and keep track of progress across the company.

  • Managers

    Set goals for their team at the start of the period and score them at the end.

  • Approvers

    The manager's manager, Sales Ops or Finance, who check goals and scores before they count.

  • Payees

    The people being scored, who see their goals and final scores.

How customers ran MBOs before

We started by studying how customers already ran MBOs. The process worked, but it lived entirely outside Everstage:

The steps themselves made sense: leadership set the goals, managers scored them, and often someone checked before payout. So I designed the product to follow those same steps, and remove the chasing and copy-pasting around them.

The core challenge

Unlike regular goal tracking, MBO scores directly affect how much someone gets paid. And they're given by the person's own manager, who naturally wants their team to do well.

Different companies also follow different processes for setting goals, reviewing scores and approving them.

So the challenge was to

support these different workflows, while making it clear who can make changes, what needs approval, and when everything is final.

It had to be flexible for companies, without becoming complicated for the people using it every day.

Three ideas helped, each mirroring a step customers already followed:

  1. Two stages. Agree on goals first, score them later.
  2. Checked, then locked. Someone else approves, and approved values can't change.
  3. Only approved values reach pay. Drafts and pending scores never touch a payout.

How it works

01Two stages: goals first, scores later

Every MBO period, whether it's monthly, quarterly, half-yearly or annual, has two moments: agreeing on goals at the start, and scoring them at the end.

Our first version used one form that could be edited anytime, so a manager could quietly change a goal at the end of the period to match the result. We split it into two stages, Goals and Scores, each with its own approver. Small fields, like a note or a date, sit outside both and can be edited anytime.

Alex’s goal

“Run 10 product demos” is agreed in January and scored in March.

We kept it to two stages because that's how the customers we knew ran MBOs. But the design and backend are flexible, so more stages can be added later.

02Checked, then locked

Many customers already had someone, like the manager's manager or Finance, check scores before payout. We built this in as an approval step for each stage, a maker-checker setup that keeps scores honest.

Once approved, a stage locks and can't be edited. It also locks on a date the admin sets, which gives managers a real deadline. Whichever comes first wins.

Alex’s goal

Approved and locked in January, so in March Alex is scored against exactly that goal.

03Only approved values reach pay

Publishing a program creates a datasheet, and only approved values go into it. Commission plans read from it to work out pay.

If a score is sent back for another review, the last approved value stays, so no one's pay disappears midway.

Alex’s goal

Jordan scores 85, Finance approves, and the plan pays Alex $500.

The messy parts

Setting goals and scores was the easy part. The harder part was handling different rules, approvals, and things not going to plan.

For managers and employees, the experience had to make a few things clear:

What should I do next? Can I still make changes? Who am I waiting on?

  1. 01Different rules per company

    Every company sets goals, approves scores and locks changes its own way. We had to support that without making everyday use complicated.

  2. 02Everyone at a different stage

    One employee's goals may be approved while another's are still being edited. Managers needed to see everyone's progress and act on several at once.

  3. 03After approval or rejection

    Approvals decide what people can still edit and when work is final. A rejection reopens only the relevant stage.

  4. 04Safer bulk actions

    Some records may not allow changes. Managers see which ones will update and which need attention before going ahead.

  5. 05Saving without submitting

    Managers can save progress, including imported data, and come back later. Submitting is a separate step, so they can review first.

Designing the experience

Four people take part, and each only cares about one part of the cycle. So MBO is split into separate submodules, and each person works in their own.

MBO programsAdmins set up a program

A guided, step-by-step setup: the period, the people, the goal form, and how each stage is approved and locked. Lock rules read in plain words, like “3 days after the period starts”, and issues like a missing manager show up before publishing.

Team MBOManagers score their team

Managers only see the people they score. Each stage is a band in the grid with its status and deadline, like “Locks on approval or Jan 20, 2026”. Drafts stay private until submitted.

ApprovalsApprovers review

Approvers see a snapshot of exactly what was submitted. They approve, or send it back with a comment. Approval is per person, so one rejection doesn't hold up the team.

My MBO and Org-wide MBOPayees and admins see the results

  • My MBO

    Payees see their goals and official scores, never a draft. Before, they only saw a number on their statement.

  • Org-wide MBO

    Admins check if they're ready to pay: spot gaps, fix values, unlock a stage for a late manager, and see who changed what.

What changed

MBOs now live in one place, from setting goals to payout.

What I took away

With no existing screens to improve, the hard part was understanding a process that lived in spreadsheets, and deciding what to keep.

Since every number ends up in someone's pay, most of my time went into what's a draft, what's final, who can change it, and what happens when work is half done or takes an unexpected path.
