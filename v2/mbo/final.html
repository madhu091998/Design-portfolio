Bringing non-quota goals into commission pay

My role
End-to-end product design
Collaborators
PM · Engineers

MBO (Management by Objectives) lets companies on Everstage reward goals that aren't a quota number, like a certification, a launch milestone or a manager's quarterly scorecard.

I designed the module from scratch, from setting up a program to managers scoring their teams, approvals, and what payees and admins see. A key focus was making sure only reviewed, final values ever reach someone's pay.

Who uses MBO?

  • Admins

    Comp and RevOps teams who design the goal form, decide who's in it, set deadlines and approvals, and watch progress across the org.

  • Managers

    People who fill in goals and scores for their team, quickly and without breaking anything.

  • Approvers

    People who review what managers submitted and approve or reject it.

  • Payees

    People earning on MBOs, more often managers and leaders than reps, who see their own goals and final scores, never a half-finished draft.

How companies managed MBOs before

MBOs are structured goals set for employees, usually by their managers. Each goal has a weight or score, and the total decides part of their variable pay. Sometimes it's an extra bonus on top. The goals are often qualitative, and tracked each period:

  • Certifications

    Getting a product certification.

  • Milestones

    Hitting a strategic milestone or a launch-readiness goal.

  • Activity goals

    Running a set number of customer-success calls.

  • Scorecards

    A manager's quarterly scorecard on demo quality.

Several Everstage customers already ran MBOs internally, but there was no easy way to manage them in Everstage. Every period looked something like this:

  1. 1
    Managers defined goals manually.

    For each of their reportees, outside Everstage.

  2. 2
    Admins collected them one by one.

    Reaching out to each manager individually for goals and scores across all their reportees.

  3. 3
    Everything went into a CSV.

    Consolidated by hand and uploaded into Everstage each quarter.

  4. 4
    The data was fed into pay.

    Through a custom object and a datasheet, then into the commission engine for payout.

Admins had become middlemen, chasing managers for goals and scores, and repeating it at the start and end of every period.

Where it hurt

  • Manual collection. Admins chased managers to get goals and scores for each payee.
  • The same effort, every period. It was repeated at the beginning and end of every quarter or half year.
  • No visibility. Managers had nowhere to enter or review goal progress. Payees couldn't see their goals or how they were doing, and often only saw the final MBO payout in their statements.
  • No structured reminders. Admins followed up by hand, which led to delays and inconsistencies.
  • No audit trail. Since it all happened outside the platform, there was no record of who approved or updated what.

Shaping the product

Understanding how MBOs work

A few patterns stood out in how companies run MBOs:

  • Top-down

    Leadership sets the framework. Managers add weightage and scores.

  • A smaller share of pay

    Usually around 10 to 35% of the payout.

  • Their own rhythm

    Monthly, quarterly or annual, separate from commissions.

  • Mostly for managers

    To keep subjectivity out of reps' pay.

But this raised a question:

If managers score their own team, how do we keep scores fair?

The answer was a maker-checker process: managers enter scores, and someone else, like their manager or sales ops, signs off. Since who approves varies by company, approvals had to be configurable.

What we set out to build

The goal was to give companies one place to define a goal, have managers score it each period, and send the final values straight into pay, with clear ownership, deadlines, and a formal sign-off when needed. All with minimal manual collection and back-and-forth for managers.

For example:

Jordan rates Alex on a quarterly demo scorecard. The plan says "score 80 or higher pays a $500 bonus." Jordan submits 85, so Alex earns $500.

Captured once, consistent, and tied to pay. Getting there happened in two phases.

01Templates attached to commission plans

In the first version, admins built a template (the goal form) and attached it to a commission plan. Publishing the plan created one goal row per payee, per period. Managers filled in rows with a save draft and submit model, and submitted values flowed to pay.

It came with three views: My MBO for payees, Team MBO for managers, and Org-wide MBO for admins.

02MBO as its own program, with approvals

In the second phase, MBO became a self-contained program. It owns its own period, participants, managers, stages and approvals, with no commission plan needed in between.

Programs added stages, optional approval workflows and field locking. The MBO manager could be someone other than the reporting manager, and final values sync to a datasheet that commission formulas can read, only after approval.

Since these values drive real pay, one question shaped every screen after that:

How do we make sure only reviewed, final values ever reach a payout?

Designing the experience

01Setting up a program

Admins create a program through a guided, step-by-step setup in a side-nav layout. On a draft, the side nav shows progress and admins move with Next and Back. After publishing, they can jump to any step.

  1. 1
    Create program

    Name, period, and frequency: monthly, quarterly, half-yearly or annual.

  2. 2
    Manage participants

    Pick people from the employee list with filters like commission plan, manager, group, joining date and designation.

  3. 3
    Build the form

    The fields managers fill in, with read-only context like deal size or segment shown next to them.

  4. 4
    Stages, approvals and locking

    Optional. Group fields into stages, and add approval workflows and lock dates.

  5. 5
    Notifications and notes

    Reminders for managers, plus rich-text guidance like a scoring rubric.

  6. 6
    Publish

    The program goes live for managers right away, and a datasheet is created for commission formulas.

02Choosing who's in it

Each participant gets an MBO manager, defaulting to their reporting manager. People can also join for part of the period, like mid-year joiners.

Instead of failing on save, a Validation column flags problems inline. A missing manager shows as a warning, and a person who isn't eligible or a manager who's leaving shows as an error.

03Building the goal form

The form builder sets up the fields managers fill in: the field type, whether it's required, help text, min and max values, dropdown options and restricted fields.

Admins can also add read-only context fields, like deal size or segment, shown next to the goals so managers have what they need to score.

04Stages, approvals and locking

A period has two real moments: setting goals at the start and scoring them at the end. So admins can group fields into up to two stages, like Goals and Scores. The second stage only opens once the first is done.

  • Approval workflows

    Each stage can use an approval workflow, from Level 1 to Level 2 and beyond.

  • Locking

    Each stage can lock on a date, like "3 days after the period starts", or on approval, whichever comes first.

Admins also choose how submitting works: as each person's stage is ready, or only once the whole team is complete, which means fewer approval requests.

05Filling in and submitting goals

Managers open Team MBO, pick a program and land on the current period. A status summary shows who's not started, started or completed, and an Approvals requiring attention card appears when stages need sign-off. They can switch between their direct and extended team.

  1. 1
    Edit in place

    Each cell's editor matches the field: a dropdown, date picker, long-text pop-out or file upload.

  2. 2
    Save as draft anytime

    Drafts are private. Payees and pay don't see them, and a "Records with drafts" filter helps find unfinished work.

  3. 3
    Review before submitting

    Submit opens a review showing what will submit now and what stays a draft, like a stage that isn't ready yet.

For big teams, managers can bulk edit or paste from a spreadsheet.

06Approving a stage

When a manager submits a stage, a request appears in Approvals for the approvers. Each goal moves through a simple path:

  1. 1
    Draft

    The manager is still working on it.

  2. 2
    Submitted

    Waiting for approval. The manager can revoke it to make changes before it's reviewed.

  3. 3
    Approved

    The stage locks and, for Goals, the Scores stage opens up.

  4. 4
    Or rejected

    A comment is required. Values go back to draft, with the comment shown on the row.

Approvers see a snapshot of the values and files at the moment of submit, so what they approve is exactly what was submitted. And approval moves per person: if 9 are approved and 1 is rejected, the 9 move on.

07What payees and admins see

  • My MBO

    Payees see their goals per period and who their MBO manager is. Only official values show, never a draft or a stage still waiting for approval.

  • Org-wide MBO

    Admins see every participant by reporting hierarchy, filter by status or people without MBOs, fill in values when a manager isn't available, and view the full history of any row.

How a score reaches pay

MBO programs and commission plans stay separate, and a datasheet links the two. When a program is published, it creates its own datasheet. Admins point a commission plan at it like any other data source, and the plan's formula turns the score into money.

Why it works this way

  • A datasheet in between

    Programs work on their own, and any plan can use the data.

  • Only approved values

    Finance can trust that pay is based on reviewed numbers.

  • The last approved value stays

    A resubmission never wipes out someone's pay mid-review.

  • A warning, not a block

    Locking a payout flags unfinished MBOs before money goes out.

Designing for the messy cases

Real teams change during a period. People join midway, managers leave, and deadlines pass with work still open. We designed for each of these instead of assuming every period would go to plan.

01When the MBO manager changes

A skip-level manager might score someone, or a manager might be leaving.

Admins pick the new manager, then an "Effective from" date. A timeline shows past, current and upcoming managers, without touching the reporting hierarchy.

The system blocks dates the new manager can't cover, like after their exit, and explains why.

02When a re-submission is pending

Payees keep seeing the last approved value until the new one is approved, so they never see a number that might still change.

03When a deadline passes with work still open

Only admins can unlock a date-locked stage, for one manager or all of them, so managers can edit again.

04When payouts are locked with open MBOs

An admin might lock a commission statement while that person still has an unfinished MBO period.

The product warns them, links straight to the open period in Org-wide MBO, and offers an Excel list of everyone with pending or incomplete goals.

Key decisions along the way

01Draft and submit, everywhere

"Save freely, submit when sure" removed the fear of touching a grid that drives pay. Payees only ever see official values.

02Two stages, built to grow

The UI shows Goals and Scores, the two real moments in a period, while the model supports more stages later. Fields outside stages, like an MBO date, stay simple and skip approvals, so not every edit needs a sign-off.

03Fast submitting or fewer approval requests?

Submitting as each person is ready is faster. Waiting for the whole team means less noise for approvers.

This was a real trade-off customers asked to choose, so we offered both.

04Lock on approval or date, whichever comes first

Managers get a deadline that still respects approvals, and only admins can unlock.

05Errors where you work

Eligibility and manager problems show inline in the Validation column with clear banners, and Publish explains why it's disabled, instead of failing on save.

What it made possible

Companies now have one place to define, score, approve and pay on non-quota goals, replacing spreadsheets and email.

  • A clear trail

    Who entered, changed and approved every value.

  • Confidence in payouts

    Approvals and locks give finance confidence that only reviewed numbers reach payouts.

What I took away

Building MBO from scratch meant there was no existing screen to improve. Every part, from setup to approvals, had to be shaped around how companies actually run a period.

Because these values decide what people get paid, I spent a lot of time on what's a draft and what's final, and on who can see and change what, and when.

That's all for this one.
