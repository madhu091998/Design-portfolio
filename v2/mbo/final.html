Bringing non-quota goals into commission pay

My role
End-to-end product design
Collaborators

Type
New module, built from scratch

Sales teams don't only pay for revenue. Many companies also reward things like getting certified, hitting a launch milestone or running good demos. These are called MBOs, and Everstage customers were managing them in spreadsheets and long email threads.

I owned the design of the MBO module end to end, working closely with our PM and engineers. It lets companies set these goals, have managers score them, get the scores reviewed and pay on them, all without leaving Everstage. Because these scores turn into real money, a big part of my work was making sure the numbers people get paid on are fair and final.

What's an MBO?

MBO stands for Management by Objectives. These are goals that can't be measured as a sales number, so a manager scores them instead, usually once a quarter. That score then decides a part of the person's payout, typically somewhere between 10 and 35%.

To keep things simple, I'll follow one example through this case study.

Alex's goal this quarter is to run 10 product demos. At the end of the quarter, Jordan, Alex's manager, gives it a score. If the score is 80 or above, Alex earns a $500 bonus.

How customers ran MBOs before

Before designing anything, we studied how customers were already running MBOs, step by step. Most of them had a process that worked. It just happened entirely outside Everstage, and every quarter it looked roughly like this:

  1. 1
    Managers set and scored goals in Excel or over email.
  2. 2
    Admins followed up with every manager, one by one, and pasted it all into a single CSV.
  3. 3
    The CSV was uploaded into Everstage, where it fed into the commission calculation.

Admins had turned into middlemen. They did this at the start and end of every quarter, nobody could tell which scores were final, and the people being scored rarely saw more than a number on their statement.

What stood out was that the process itself wasn't broken. Leadership decided the goals, managers scored their teams, and often someone else checked the numbers before pay went out. So instead of inventing a new way of working, I wanted the product to follow the same steps customers already trusted, and take away the chasing and copy-pasting around them.

The core challenge

One thing made this harder than a regular form. These scores turn directly into money, and they're given by the person's own manager, who naturally wants their team to do well.

So the question I kept coming back to was:

How do we make sure the score someone gets paid on is fair, reviewed and final?

Three ideas came out of looking at how customers handled this offline. Each one mirrors a step they were already following:

  1. Two stages. Customers agreed on goals at the start of the quarter and scored them at the end, so the product works the same way.
  2. Checked, then locked. Many already had someone review the scores. We built that in, and made sure an approved number can't be edited afterwards.
  3. Only approved values reach pay. Drafts and scores still under review never touch a payout.

How it works

01Two stages: goals first, scores later

When we looked closely, an MBO quarter wasn't one moment but two. At the start, the manager and the person agree on what the goals are. At the end, the manager scores how well each one went.

Our first version used a single form that could be edited at any time, and that left a real gap. A manager could quietly change a goal in March to match what had actually happened. So I split the form into two stages, Goals and Scores, each reviewed by the right person.

Alex’s goal

“Run 10 product demos” is agreed in the first week of January, in the Goals stage. Jordan only scores it at the end of March, in the Scores stage.

We kept it to two stages because the customers we knew only ran MBOs in these two moments, and more stages would have meant extra setup for almost nobody. That said, we built the design and the backend so that more stages can be added later without a rework.

02Checked, then locked

Many customers already had someone, like the manager's manager or a person from Finance, look over the scores before they were paid. We built that in as an approval step for each stage. This maker-checker setup keeps scores from being inflated.

Approval alone wasn't enough, though. If an approved score could still be edited, the approval didn't mean much. So once a stage is approved, it locks. It also locks on a date the admin picks, which gives managers a real deadline instead of admins having to chase them. Whichever happens first wins.

Alex’s goal

Jordan's manager approves the goal in early January, and it locks. So when March comes around, Alex is scored against exactly what was agreed.

03Only approved values reach pay

In the first version, MBO lived inside a commission plan, which tied it to that plan's setup. We pulled it out into its own program and connected the two through a datasheet. Publishing a program creates one, and any commission plan can read from it like any other data.

Only approved values ever reach that datasheet. If a score goes back for another review, the last approved value stays in place, so someone's pay doesn't disappear in the middle of it.

Alex’s goal

Jordan scores Alex 85 and Finance approves it. Within minutes the datasheet updates, and the commission plan pays Alex the $500 bonus.

Designing the experience

Four kinds of people take part in an MBO cycle, and each of them only cares about a small part of it. So rather than one big screen for everyone, I gave each person their own place in the product.

MBO programsAdmins set up a program

Setting up a program is a guided, step-by-step flow: the period, who's included, the goal form, and how each stage is approved and locked. I wrote lock rules in plain words, like “3 days after the period starts”, instead of a toggle or a raw date. If something is off, like a person without a manager, it shows up right there, before anything goes live.

Team MBOManagers score their team

Managers only see the people they're responsible for scoring. Each stage appears as its own band in the grid, with its status and deadline, for example “Locks on approval or Jan 20, 2026”, so a lock never catches anyone off guard. They can save drafts as often as they like, and nobody else sees a draft until it's submitted.

ApprovalsApprovers review

Approvers see a snapshot of exactly what the manager submitted, so the numbers can't shift while they're reviewing. They can approve, or send it back with a comment. Approval works person by person, so one questionable score doesn't hold up the rest of the team.

My MBO and Org-wide MBOPayees and admins see the results

  • My MBO

    Payees can finally see their goals and official scores, never a half-finished draft. Before this, most of them only saw a final number on their statement.

  • Org-wide MBO

    Admins get one screen to answer “are we ready to pay?”. They can spot gaps, fill in or fix values, unlock a stage for a manager who's running late, and see who changed what.

What changed

MBOs now live in one place. Admins set things up once, managers score inside Everstage, someone reviews the numbers, and only approved scores flow into pay. No more spreadsheets or chasing people over email.

What I took away

This was the first time I designed something with no existing screens to improve on. The tricky part wasn't the UI. It was understanding a process that lived in spreadsheets and people's heads, and deciding which parts of it were worth keeping.

Because every number in MBO ends up in someone's paycheck, I spent most of my time on the less visible questions: is this a draft or final, who's allowed to change it, and when does it stop changing.

That's all for this one.
