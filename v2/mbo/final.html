New module, built from scratch

Sales teams don't only pay for revenue. Many also reward goals like getting certified or running good demos, called MBOs. Everstage customers managed these in spreadsheets and email.

I owned the design of the MBO module end to end, with our PM and engineers. It lets companies set goals, have managers score them, review the scores and pay on them, all in Everstage. Since scores turn into real money, my focus was making sure people are paid on fair, final numbers.

What's an MBO?

MBO stands for Management by Objectives. These are goals that can't be measured as a sales number, so a manager scores them, usually each quarter. The score decides part of the person's payout, typically 10 to 35%.

One example runs through this case study:

Alex's goal is to run 10 product demos this quarter. Jordan, Alex's manager, scores it at the end. A score of 80 or more pays a $500 bonus.

How customers ran MBOs before

We started by studying how customers already ran MBOs. The process worked, but it lived entirely outside Everstage:

The steps themselves made sense: leadership set the goals, managers scored them, and often someone checked before payout. So I designed the product to follow those same steps, and remove the chasing and copy-pasting around them.

The core challenge

These scores become real money, and they come from the person's own manager, who naturally wants their team to do well.

The question I kept asking:

How do we make sure people are paid on scores that are fair, reviewed and final?

Three ideas answer it, each mirroring a step customers already followed:

  1. Two stages. Agree on goals first, score them later.
  2. Checked, then locked. Someone else approves, and approved values can't change.
  3. Only approved values reach pay. Drafts and pending scores never touch a payout.

How it works

01Two stages: goals first, scores later

An MBO quarter has two moments: agreeing on goals at the start, and scoring them at the end.

Our first version used one form that could be edited anytime, so a manager could quietly change a goal in March to match the result. I split it into two stages, Goals and Scores, each with its own approver.

Alex’s goal

“Run 10 product demos” is agreed in January and scored in March.

We kept it to two stages because that's how the customers we knew ran MBOs. But the design and backend are flexible, so more stages can be added later.

02Checked, then locked

Many customers already had someone, like the manager's manager or Finance, check scores before payout. We built this in as an approval step for each stage, a maker-checker setup that keeps scores honest.

Once approved, a stage locks and can't be edited. It also locks on a date the admin sets, which gives managers a real deadline. Whichever comes first wins.

Alex’s goal

Approved and locked in January, so in March Alex is scored against exactly that goal.

03Only approved values reach pay

MBO used to live inside a commission plan. We made it its own program, linked to pay through a datasheet that any commission plan can read.

Only approved values reach the datasheet. During a re-review, the last approved value stays, so pay never disappears midway.

Alex’s goal

Jordan scores 85, Finance approves, and the plan pays Alex $500.

Designing the experience

Four people take part, and each only cares about one part of the cycle. So each gets their own place in the product.

MBO programsAdmins set up a program

A guided, step-by-step setup: the period, the people, the goal form, and how each stage is approved and locked. Lock rules read in plain words, like “3 days after the period starts”, and issues like a missing manager show up before publishing.

Team MBOManagers score their team

Managers only see the people they score. Each stage is a band in the grid with its status and deadline, like “Locks on approval or Jan 20, 2026”. Drafts stay private until submitted.

ApprovalsApprovers review

Approvers see a snapshot of exactly what was submitted. They approve, or send it back with a comment. Approval is per person, so one rejection doesn't hold up the team.

My MBO and Org-wide MBOPayees and admins see the results

  • My MBO

    Payees see their goals and official scores, never a draft. Before, they only saw a number on their statement.

  • Org-wide MBO

    Admins check if they're ready to pay: spot gaps, fix values, unlock a stage for a late manager, and see who changed what.

What changed

MBOs now live in one place, from setting goals to payout.

What I took away

With no existing screens to improve, the hard part was understanding a process that lived in spreadsheets, and deciding what to keep.

Since every number ends up in someone's pay, most of my time went into what's a draft, what's final, and who can change it.
